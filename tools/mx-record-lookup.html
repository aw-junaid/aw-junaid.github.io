✦ How This Tool Works

How does a lookup work without a backend server?

Every lookup is made directly from your browser to a public DNS-over-HTTPS resolver (Google or Cloudflare, whichever you pick above) using a plain HTTPS request. Nothing about the domains you look up passes through this site's own infrastructure \u2014 only the resolver you choose sees the query, the same as any other DNS request.

What does "implicit MX" mean, and why does it matter?

RFC 5321 says that if a domain has no MX records at all, mail servers should fall back to delivering directly to that domain's A/AAAA record. A domain with genuinely no mail setup and a domain that's silently relying on this fallback look identical unless you check for it \u2014 this toggle checks the A record specifically when MX comes back empty, so you're not misled into thinking mail is completely unconfigured.

How is the mail provider detected?

The hostname of each returned MX record is matched against a list of known fingerprints \u2014 things like aspmx.l.google.com for Google Workspace, *.protection.outlook.com for Microsoft 365, or *.pphosted.com for Proofpoint. It's a best-effort match against public naming conventions, not an authoritative source, so treat it as a strong hint rather than a certainty for less common or self-hosted setups.

Why check SPF and DMARC alongside MX?

MX records tell you where mail is delivered to; SPF and DMARC tell you whether mail claiming to be from that domain can be trusted. A real deliverability or security audit checks all three together \u2014 a domain can have perfectly valid MX records and still be wide open to spoofing if SPF is missing or DMARC has no enforcement policy (p=none).

Why does the bulk audit add a delay between domains?

Public DoH resolvers apply rate limits per client. Running dozens of lookups back-to-back with no delay risks getting temporarily throttled partway through a list, which would silently produce incomplete results. The configurable delay keeps a large audit reliable at the cost of taking a bit longer.

What counts as a "match" in the migration verifier?

Each expected host you list is checked as a case-insensitive substring against every live MX record's hostname \u2014 so entering aspmx.l.google.com matches even if the live record has a different priority number attached. This mirrors how you'd eyeball a migration checklist: you care that the right host shows up somewhere in the live records, not its exact position.
