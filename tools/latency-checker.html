Network Latency Checker

The real-world problem: "my internet feels slow" is hard to prove and harder to diagnose from a single ping. This runs repeated timed requests to compare multiple endpoints and find the fastest one, monitors a single target continuously to catch intermittent slowdowns a one-off test would miss, and measures jitter and packet loss over a burst of requests — then checks the numbers against the thresholds that actually matter for gaming, VoIP, and video calls.

✦ How This Tool Works

Why isn't this a real ping (ICMP)?

Browsers are sandboxed from raw sockets — a webpage cannot send an ICMP echo request the way the command-line ping does. Instead this times how long an HTTP request takes to complete, which includes DNS lookup, TCP handshake, and (depending on the target) TLS negotiation. It's a heavier measurement than ICMP ping, but it reflects what actually matters for browsing, calls, and most online games, which all ride on top of that same stack.

Why discard the first request as a "warm-up"?

The first request to a host pays for DNS resolution and connection setup that later requests reuse (keep-alive, cached DNS). Counting it would inflate the average with a cost that isn't representative of the ongoing connection quality — which is what you're usually trying to measure.

What's the difference between average latency and jitter?

Average latency is how fast a connection is; jitter is how consistent it is. A connection averaging 40ms but swinging between 10ms and 150ms will stutter on a video call even though its average looks great — jitter is often the better predictor of a "choppy" experience than the average alone.

Why p95 instead of the maximum?

A single freak spike (a background download kicking in, a Wi-Fi hiccup) can make the maximum look far worse than the connection typically behaves. The 95th percentile — the value 95% of requests beat — describes the realistic worst case without letting one outlier dominate the read.

Why do gaming and VoIP get such strict thresholds?

Real-time interactive traffic is far more sensitive to delay and jitter than loading a webpage is: competitive gaming generally wants well under 60ms with minimal jitter, and voice/video calls start to feel laggy or start dropping frames well before general browsing would notice a slowdown. Streaming and page loads tolerate much higher latency because buffering hides it.

Is anything sent to a server?

Only the timing requests to whichever URLs you enter — that's the measurement itself. All timing, statistics, jitter/loss calculation, and grading against thresholds happen locally in your browser. Your target lists and settings only persist in this browser's local storage.
