✦ How This Tool Works

What problem does this actually solve?

Whether you're reading nmap output, writing a firewall rule, or investigating a suspicious connection in a log, you constantly need to know "what is port X for, and should it worry me?" This tool answers both parts at once — service identification plus a practical risk read, without switching between five different reference pages.

Where does the port data come from?

The database mirrors the well-known and commonly-registered service assignments historically published by IANA (Internet Assigned Numbers Authority) for TCP, UDP, and SCTP, supplemented with widely recognized de-facto ports (like 8080 for HTTP proxies) that are heavily used in practice even without a formal universal registration.

What do Well-Known, Registered, and Dynamic mean?

Ports 0–1023 are "Well-Known" and traditionally require elevated privileges to bind on Unix-like systems. Ports 1024–49151 are "Registered," assignable to specific services on request but not privilege-restricted. Ports 49152–65535 are "Dynamic" or "Ephemeral," used for temporary client-side connections and not meant to be permanently assigned to any service.

How does the risk flagging work?

Each port in the database carries independent tags — insecure/legacy protocol, remote-access service, database service, or commonly malware-associated — and you can toggle which categories to surface. This isn't a live threat feed; it reflects well-documented historical associations (e.g. port 4444 with Metasploit's default handler) meant to prompt a closer look, not a definitive verdict on any specific open port.

Can I paste raw nmap or firewall output?

Paste one port per line — plain numbers, "port/protocol" pairs, or ranges like 8080-8090 all work, and the parser ignores anything it can't recognize as a port entry. For full nmap grep-able output with extra columns, extract just the port column first; this tool focuses on the port list itself rather than parsing arbitrary scan formats.

Is anything sent to a server?

No. The entire port database, search, batch processing, and risk classification run locally in your browser from a bundled dataset — nothing you type is ever sent anywhere. Your draft and settings only persist in this browser's local storage.

Why would a service have "related ports"?

Some protocols use more than one port together — FTP's control channel on 21 works alongside a separate data channel, and DNS uses 53 for both queries and zone transfers with different behavior on TCP vs UDP. Surfacing related ports helps you recognize when you're looking at pieces of the same service rather than unrelated open ports.
