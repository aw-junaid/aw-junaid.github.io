✦ How This Tool Works

What problem does this actually solve?

ROT13 shows up constantly in the real world — forum and Usenet spoiler tags, puzzle hunts, CTF challenges, and old-school "don't read this unless you want it spoiled" text. But the moment a puzzle uses a shift other than 13, or mixes in digits, most quick tools break. This one handles arbitrary Caesar shifts, case preservation, digit rotation, ROT47's full symbol range, Atbash, and — critically — figuring out the shift when you don't already know it.

Why is ROT13 its own inverse but Caesar shift 3 isn't?

The Latin alphabet has 26 letters. Rotating by exactly half that (13) means applying the same rotation twice lands back on the original letter, so encoding and decoding are the identical operation. Any other shift needs its mirror image (26 − N) to reverse it, which is why this tool separates "Force encode" from "Force decode" for non-13 shifts.

What's ROT47 and when would I use it?

ROT47 rotates the full printable ASCII range (33–126) by 47 places instead of just the 26 letters, so it also scrambles digits, punctuation, and symbols. It's common in Usenet posts and some obfuscated scripts because it hides more of the original structure than plain ROT13, though it's exactly as easy to reverse.

How does auto-crack guess the shift without a key?

It decodes the ciphertext under all 25 possible shifts, measures each candidate's letter frequency distribution against known English letter frequencies using a chi-squared-style distance score, and surfaces the shift whose output looks most like real English. Short or non-English text can fool this, so always sanity-check the result yourself.

Is ROT13 or Atbash actually secure?

No — none of the ciphers here provide real security. They have no secret key beyond a small, guessable number (or none at all, for Atbash), and can be broken instantly by brute force or frequency analysis, as this very tool demonstrates. They're obfuscation for spoilers and puzzles, not encryption for anything sensitive.

Is anything sent to a server?

No. Every computation — encoding, decoding, batch processing, brute-force rotation, and frequency scoring — runs entirely in your browser. Nothing you type ever leaves the page.
