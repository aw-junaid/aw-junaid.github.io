✦ How This Tool Works

What problem does this actually solve?

Real Caesar-cipher problems rarely come with a stated key. CTF challenges, escape-room puzzles, and old obfuscated logs hand you ciphertext and nothing else. This tool covers both directions: encrypt/decrypt when you know the key, and auto-crack via frequency analysis plus common-word scoring when you don't — with a full ranked table of all 26 shifts so you can eyeball the runner-up guesses too.

How does the auto-crack actually guess the key?

It shifts the ciphertext by every possible amount, then scores each candidate with a chi-squared test comparing its letter distribution to known English letter frequencies (E, T, A, O... are common; Z, Q, X are rare). Real English text scores low (close match); random-looking shifts score high. Common-word matching then breaks close ties.

Why would I rotate digits too?

Plenty of real obfuscation schemes — serial numbers, simple product-key scramblers, some puzzle-hunt ciphers — rotate 0–9 alongside letters using the same shift. Enabling digit rotation applies an independent 0–9 ring shifted by the same key, without disturbing your letter alphabet.

What's the difference between "preserve" and "strip" for non-alphabet characters?

Preserve keeps spaces, punctuation, and numbers exactly where they are in the output — this is the historically accurate behavior and what most puzzles expect. Strip removes anything outside your defined alphabet entirely, which is occasionally useful for producing a clean unbroken letter stream, e.g. for further cipher chaining.

Is Caesar cipher actually secure?

No — it has at most as many keys as your alphabet has characters (26 for standard English), so it's trivially broken by brute force or frequency analysis, as this tool demonstrates. It has no place protecting real secrets; treat it as a teaching tool, puzzle mechanic, or lightweight obfuscation at best.

Is anything sent to a server?

No. Every computation — encryption, decryption, brute-forcing, frequency analysis, batch processing, and key generation — runs entirely in your browser. Nothing you type ever leaves the page.
