✦ How This Tool Works

What problem does this actually solve?

Broadcast address math trips people up constantly — subtracting a wildcard mask by hand, remembering that /31 links have no broadcast, or figuring out why a "spare" address at the top of a subnet can't be assigned to a host. This tool computes the broadcast address instantly from a CIDR, dotted mask, or wildcard mask, alongside the network address and usable range, and flags the edge cases before they become an outage.

How is the broadcast address calculated?

The broadcast address is the network address with every host bit set to 1 — equivalently, the network address OR'd with the wildcard mask (the inverse of the subnet mask). It's the last address in the subnet's range and is reserved; it can't be assigned to a host.

Why don't /31 and /32 have a broadcast address?

A /32 is a single host route with no subnet to broadcast into. A /31 has exactly two addresses, and RFC 3021 assigns both to point-to-point hosts rather than reserving one as a broadcast address — a convention widely used on router-to-router links to avoid wasting addresses.

What does "directed broadcast" mean, and why the warning?

A directed broadcast is a packet sent to a subnet's broadcast address from outside that subnet. Most routers disable forwarding directed broadcasts by default (RFC 2644) because they were historically abused for amplification attacks like Smurf — worth knowing before relying on one for legitimate discovery traffic.

What does the VLSM planner actually compute?

Given a base network and a list of host-count requirements, it sorts them largest to smallest, finds the smallest prefix that satisfies each requirement's usable-host count, and carves out non-overlapping subnets from the base block in order — the standard manual VLSM allocation approach, automated.

Is anything sent to a server?

No, with one exception: the "Detect My Network's Broadcast" button is opt-in and calls a third-party API (ipify.org) to look up your public IP. Everything else — parsing, mask conversion, broadcast/network math, VLSM planning, batch auditing — runs entirely in your browser.
